A shelf is the most expensive square meter in a store

Every planogram decides which products get space and how much. Too few facings for fast movers means empty shelves and lost sales; too many for slow movers means stock that gets marked down again and again. The platform had one job — make every shelf meter earn — so I measured design decisions against sales per shelf meter and inventory on hand.

Problem

Merchandise placement was handled by office staff with no planning or analytics experience. Existing tools assumed expertise they didn't have, so layouts were built on gut feeling:

  • Inefficient space — overloaded zones and shelves limit product accessibility.
  • Disorganized categories — frequently bought items are spread out, slowing down shopping.
  • Poor placement — high-margin and popular items end up in weak positions.
  • No room for change — new arrivals and edits break the layout every time.

Key insight

Users don't read instructions — they try things.

So the interface had to guide and check the user by itself, instead of relying on training.

Mapping the work before drawing screens

35+ screens started as one user flow: access requests, categories, planograms, exports, reports and logs. Mapping it first let the team agree on scope and dependencies before a single pixel was drawn — and later made every new feature land in the right place.

Solution

Move the expertise into the interface

Instead of teaching staff to become planners, I built three things into the product: a visual editor, validation in place of training, and guidance at the moment of action.

01 · Visual editor

See the shelf, not a spreadsheet

Planograms are edited on a visual shelf: drag products, change facings, open any item to set its size, value and tags. Staff work with what they see in the store, not with product codes.

Why
Staff think in shelves and products, not in tables and codes. A visual editor removes the translation step where most mistakes happen.
Effect
First store-level planograms in under 2 weeks instead of 20+ for a Fortune 500 grocer.

02 · Validation

Mistakes are caught on the screen, not in the store

Every change is checked against the real shelf. Overflowing sections and height mismatches are highlighted in place with a plain-language hint, so errors that used to surface after installation are fixed before export.

Why
An error found after installation costs a store visit and lost sales. An error found on screen costs one click.
Effect
Shelf space utilisation grew from 90% to 96%, with higher shelf compliance in stores.

03 · Guidance

Onboarding inside the work, not before it

First-time users get short Quick Tips right where they act: select a store, open a mudmap, rebuild the planogram. No manual to read, no training session to book.

Why
Training sessions don't scale to thousands of stores. Guidance at the moment of action does.
Effect
Planning effort went down — teams were redeployed to vendor and strategy work.

Analytics

Every layout change shows its money effect

Financial reports connect planograms to sales, profit, stock and displaced products. Category managers see planned vs actual results and which SKUs lost space — and act on data instead of intuition.

Why
Category managers have to defend every layout change to the business. Numbers next to the shelf make that conversation short.
Effect
+17% profit uplift vs. control stores for a global health & beauty retailer.

Compare versions before rolling out

Any planogram version can be compared with the previous one by sales, units, products and days of supply, with a one-click reset for a single value. Decisions became reversible — so teams experimented more.

Why
When a change can be undone, people stop being afraid to try. Fear of mistakes was the main brake on optimization.
Effect
Unlimited planogram scenarios, with changes made in minutes instead of days.

Precision where it matters

For the physical setup, pegboards and shelves keep exact parameters — offsets, peg spacing, class and type — so what's planned is exactly what gets installed.
